<section className="explainer-section pt-10 sm:pt-14">
  <p className="section-kicker">01 · You already measure change</p>
  <h2 className="mt-3 font-heading text-4xl leading-[0.98] font-semibold tracking-[-0.035em] text-balance sm:text-5xl">
    An average is the part you already know.
  </h2>

If a car travels $75$ metres in $5$ seconds, its average speed is

$$
\frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}}
=\frac{75\ \text{m}}{5\ \text{s}}=15\ \text{m/s}.
$$

That number tells the truth about the whole interval. It does not say how fast
the car was moving at any one instant. Calculus begins when we ask what happens
to the average as the interval disappears.

</section>

<section className="explainer-section" aria-labelledby="the-one-move">
  <p className="section-kicker">02 · The one move</p>
  <AnchorHeading anchor="the-one-move">Let the gap shrink.</AnchorHeading>

At a single instant, the usual average-change formula asks for $0/0$. That is
undefined, so we do not substitute zero. We calculate with nearby, nonzero
gaps and ask whether their answers approach one number.

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
$$

The limit is the formal move that makes “change at an instant” precise. This is
why Chapter 1 spends time on limits before the derivative receives its full
definition.

<section className="explainer-section" aria-labelledby="adding-up-pieces">
  <p className="section-kicker">03 · The same move, backwards</p>
  <AnchorHeading anchor="adding-up-pieces">
    Add smaller and smaller pieces.
  </AnchorHeading>

Suppose you know an object's acceleration $a(t)$ and want its change in
velocity. Over a short time $Δt$, the change is approximately
$a(t)\Delta t$. Chop the whole time interval into pieces, add those estimates,
and shrink the widest piece:

$$
\Delta v=\lim_{\max \Delta t_i\to 0}
\sum_i a(t_i)\Delta t_i
=\int_{t_0}^{t_1} a(t)\,dt.
$$

This is not a second trick. The derivative squeezes an average until it becomes
local; the integral squeezes a sum of approximations until it becomes exact.
One limiting idea runs in two directions.

</section>

<section className="explainer-section" aria-labelledby="ftc">
  <p className="section-kicker">04 · The Fundamental Theorems</p>
  <AnchorHeading anchor="ftc">The two directions undo each other.</AnchorHeading>

One Fundamental Theorem says that differentiating an accumulation gives back
the rate being accumulated. The other says that a definite integral—defined
as a limit of sums—can be evaluated with an antiderivative:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x),
\qquad
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).
$$

We meet these results directly in Week 14. They explain why “instantaneous
change” and “adding tiny pieces” belong in one course rather than two unrelated
chapters.

</section>

<section className="explainer-section" aria-labelledby="tooling">
  <p className="section-kicker">05 · The tooling, and the point</p>
  <AnchorHeading anchor="tooling">
    Algebra is the handle, not the destination.
  </AnchorHeading>

Factoring, function notation, equations, and graphs are real requirements.
They let us hold the ideas still long enough to work with them. But a hard
algebra step is not a verdict about whether you understand calculus.

<section className="explainer-section" aria-labelledby="a-student-question">
  <p className="section-kicker">07 · A student question</p>
  <AnchorHeading anchor="a-student-question">
    Does this reach all the way to curved spacetime?
  </AnchorHeading>

<blockquote className="student-question">
  “How does finding the instantaneous slope of a curve lead down to spacetime
  being bent like water?”
</blockquote>

Finding an instantaneous slope is one version of describing what happens
locally, right where you are. In later mathematics, that local idea expands
from curves to surfaces and higher-dimensional geometry. General relativity
uses derivatives to describe how spacetime's geometry changes and how matter
moves through it. So the derivative is an early step in the language, while
“bent like water” is a useful picture rather than a literal description.
