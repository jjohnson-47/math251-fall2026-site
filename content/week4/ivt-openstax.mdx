# Continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem

A short supplement. Active Calculus does not cover the Intermediate Value
Theorem or one-sided limit notation, and both are on our course outline, so this
page fills the gap. Read it right after 1.7, while the continuity conditions are
fresh. Source: <EmbedLink href={openStaxContinuity}>OpenStax Calculus Volume 1,
Section 2.4</EmbedLink>.

<EmbedCard
  anchor="one-sided-limits"
  title="One-sided limits, and the notation for them"
  tone="note"
>

$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ is the value $f$ approaches coming from the **left**;
$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ is from the **right**.

The two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist **and
agree**. That is the precise version of condition 2 from Section 1.7, and it is
the tool you use to prove a jump discontinuity is a jump: show the two sides go
to different places.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard
  anchor="the-theorem"
  title="The Intermediate Value Theorem"
  tone="warn"
>

**If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, and $N$ is any number
between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there is at least one $c$ in $(a, b)$ with
$f(c) = N$.**

In words: a continuous function cannot get from one value to another without
passing through everything in between. It is the first _existence_ theorem in
this course — it promises that something is there without telling you how to
find it.

**Read the hypotheses, because they do the work.** Continuous, and on a
**closed** interval. Drop continuity and a jump can step straight over $N$.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="using-it" title="Using it, and the three things it does not say" tone="quiet">

To show $x^3 + x = 5$ has a solution between $1$ and $2$: let $f(x) = x^3 + x$,
note it is a polynomial and therefore continuous on $[1, 2]$, compute $f(1) = 2$
and $f(2) = 10$, and observe that $5$ lies between them. The theorem does the
rest.

**What it does not tell you:**

1. **Where.** It gives existence, not a value. Finding $c$ is a different job.
2. **How many.** “At least one” is the whole claim; there may be several.
3. **Anything at all when the hypotheses fail.** If the function is
   discontinuous, the theorem is silent — not false, silent. A solution might
   still exist.

Claiming any of those three is the standard error, precisely because saying the
theorem correctly is harder than using it.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="predict" title="Predict, then check" tone="quiet">

Answer each one in your head first, then open it.

<PredictReveal question="f is continuous on the interval from 0 to 3, with f(0) = −2 and f(3) = 4. Does f(x) = 0 have a solution strictly between 0 and 3?">

Yes, at least one. The value $0$ lies between $-2$ and $4$, and $f$ is
continuous on the closed interval, so the hypotheses hold and the theorem
applies. It still does not tell you where the solution is, and it does not tell
you there is only one.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="For f(x) = x³ + x on the interval from 1 to 2, how many solutions of f(x) = 5 does the theorem promise?">

At least one. “At least one” is the entire promise. There does happen to be
exactly one here, because $x^3 + x$ is strictly increasing, but that is a
separate argument about this particular function — the theorem never makes it
for you.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="A discontinuous function jumps straight over N. Does that make the theorem false?">

No — silent. The hypotheses fail, so the theorem makes no claim at all about
that function. Silent is not the same as false: a solution might still exist,
and finding out would take a different argument.

</PredictReveal>

</EmbedCard>

common answer, and it does not answer the question. Watch for the third omission
too: when the hypotheses fail the theorem is silent rather than false, so a
discontinuous function may still have the solution you are looking for.
