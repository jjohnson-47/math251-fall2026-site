# The Tangent Line Approximation (Section 1.8)

Here is what the derivative is _for_. Near a point, the tangent line is the best
straight-line stand-in for the function, and a straight line is something you can
evaluate in your head. This idea — replace something complicated with something
linear, close to a point — is what most of the rest of the course is built on.

<EmbedCard anchor="linearization" title="The linearization" tone="note">

$$
L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)
$$

Three pieces, and it is worth naming them: $f(a)$ is where the line starts,
$f'(a)$ is its slope, and $(x - a)$ is how far you have travelled from the point
of tangency. The arithmetic is easy. Deciding which number goes where is the
part worth practising.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard
  anchor="worked-example"
  title="Worked example: estimating ln(1.1)"
  tone="warn"
>

You know $\ln(1) = 0$. You do not know $\ln(1.1)$. The tangent line at $x = 1$
gets you close without a calculator.

**Set up.** $f(x) = \ln(x)$, and we linearize at $a = 1$ because that is the
nearby point whose value we know.

**Slope.** $f'(x) = 1/x$, so $f'(1) = 1$.

**Build the line.** $L(x) = 0 + 1 \cdot (x - 1) = x - 1$.

**Use it.** $\ln(1.1) \approx L(1.1) = 0.1$.

**Check it.** A calculator gives $\ln(1.1) \approx 0.0953$. Our estimate is
$0.1$, so we are high by about $0.0047$.

**And now the half people skip: why are we high?** Because $\ln(x)$ is concave
down, its graph bends away beneath its own tangent line, so the line sits above
the curve and every estimate from it is an over-estimate. Concavity does not
just explain the error, it tells you the _direction_ of the error before you
check. Concave down over-estimates; concave up under-estimates.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="predict" title="Predict, then check" tone="quiet">

Answer each one in your head first, then open it.

<PredictReveal question="You linearize a function that is concave up and use the line to estimate a nearby value. Is the estimate too big or too small?">

Too small. A concave-up graph bends away above its own tangent line, so the line
sits below the curve and every estimate taken from it falls short. Concave down
over-estimates; concave up under-estimates, and you can say which before you
check anything.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="Why linearize ln at a = 1 rather than at a = 1.1?">

Because $a$ has to be a point whose value you already know. $\ln(1) = 0$ is the
fact that makes the line computable, while $\ln(1.1)$ is the thing you are
trying to find — centring there would assume the answer you are after.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="The estimate of ln(1.1) was high by about 0.0047. What has happened to that error by x = 2?">

It has grown, by a lot. $L(2) = 1$ while $\ln(2) \approx 0.693$, so the error is
about $0.307$ — roughly sixty-five times the error at $1.1$. The tangent line is
the best straight-line stand-in _near_ the point of tangency, and the word
“near” is doing real work in that sentence.

</PredictReveal>

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="write-first" title="Say how concavity decides the direction" tone="quiet">

<WriteFirst
  anchor="concavity-explanation"
  prompt="Write one sentence saying how you decide, before checking, whether a tangent line estimate is too big or too small."
  selfCheck="Does your sentence name the bend in the graph, or only the answer it produced?"
>

One possible explanation: **the tangent line lies above a concave-down graph and
below a concave-up one, so concavity decides the direction of the error before
any arithmetic.** Your wording can be different.

Check that your sentence says which way the graph bends rather than which way
one estimate went. “It was too big” records what happened and does not let you
predict the next one. Watch one more limit as well: concavity gives the
direction of the error, never its size — for that you need how far $x$ is from
$a$.

Now revise your sentence until it would work for a function you have not seen
yet.

</WriteFirst>
