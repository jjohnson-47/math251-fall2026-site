# The Sine and Cosine Functions (Section 2.2)

Section 2.2 adds the first non-polynomial rules most students recognize from
graphs: the derivatives of sine and cosine. These rules matter because sine and
cosine model repeating motion, and a derivative tells you how that motion is
changing right now.

  href={activeCalculusSectionTwoTwo}
  label="Active Calculus Section 2.2: The sine and cosine functions"
/>

<EmbedCard anchor="two-rules" title="The two rules" tone="note">

The derivatives are:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin(x)\right] = \cos(x)
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\cos(x)\right] = -\sin(x)
$$

That minus sign in the second rule is not decoration. It records the way the
cosine graph is moving at the same places where sine is positive or negative.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="radians" title="Radians are required" tone="warn">

These derivative rules are true when angles are measured in radians. Calculus
uses radians because they connect angle, arc length, and rate of change without
an extra conversion factor.

If your calculator is in degrees, numerical checks for these rules will look
wrong. The graphing and derivative work in this course assumes radians unless a
problem says otherwise.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard
  anchor="unit-circle"
  title="Why the two rules fit the unit circle"
  tone="note"
>

Imagine a point moving counterclockwise around the unit circle at one radian
per unit of time. Its position is

$$
P(t) = \left(\cos(t),\sin(t)\right)
$$

The velocity points tangent to the circle. Rotating the position vector 90
degrees counterclockwise gives

$$
P'(t) = \left(-\sin(t),\cos(t)\right)
$$

Read the components separately. The horizontal position $\cos(t)$ changes at
rate $-\sin(t)$, and the vertical position $\sin(t)$ changes at rate
$\cos(t)$. The two derivative rules are the coordinate description of circular
motion.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="motion" title="What the signs mean" tone="quiet">

Think of $s(t) = \sin(t)$ as a position that moves up and down.

At $t = 0$, the position is $0$, but the graph is rising quickly. The derivative
is

$$
s'(0) = \cos(0) = 1
$$

At $t = \pi/2$, the position is at the top. The graph has flattened out before
turning back down. The derivative is

$$
s'(\pi/2) = \cos(\pi/2) = 0
$$

The derivative is not telling you where the object is. It is telling you which
way the position is moving and how fast.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard
  anchor="context"
  title="Worked example: interpret a periodic rate"
  tone="warn"
>

Suppose a rider's height is modelled by

$$
h(t) = 12 + 10\sin(t)
$$

where height is in metres and time is in minutes. Then

$$
h'(t) = 10\cos(t)
$$

At $t = \pi/3$,

$$
h'(\pi/3) = 10\cos(\pi/3) = 5
$$

The rider's vertical velocity is $5$ metres per minute upward at that instant.
The derivative is a rate with units and direction, not another height.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="cycle" title="The derivatives repeat in a four-step cycle" tone="quiet">

Starting with sine and differentiating repeatedly gives

$$
\sin(x)
\longrightarrow \cos(x)
\longrightarrow -\sin(x)
\longrightarrow -\cos(x)
\longrightarrow \sin(x)
$$

The cycle is a useful check. Four derivatives return to the function you
started with. It also keeps the negative sign attached to the derivative of
cosine.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="worked-example" title="Worked example: combine with Section 2.1" tone="plain">

Let

$$
f(x) = 3\sin(x) - 2\cos(x) + x^4
$$

Differentiate term by term.

**First term.** $3\sin(x)$ becomes $3\cos(x)$.

**Second term.** $-2\cos(x)$ becomes $-2(-\sin(x))$, which is $2\sin(x)$.

**Third term.** $x^4$ becomes $4x^3$.

So

$$
f'(x) = 3\cos(x) + 2\sin(x) + 4x^3
$$

The most common error is losing the negative sign in the derivative of cosine.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="predict" title="Predict, then check" tone="quiet">

Answer each one in your head first, then open it.

<PredictReveal question="What is the derivative of 5sin(x)?">

$5\cos(x)$. The coefficient stays out front, and the derivative of $\sin(x)$
is $\cos(x)$.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="What is the derivative of 7cos(x)?">

$-7\sin(x)$. The coefficient stays out front, and the derivative of $\cos(x)$
is $-\sin(x)$.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="If p(t) = cos(t), what is p'(0), and what does it say?">

$p'(0) = -\sin(0) = 0$. At $t = 0$, the cosine graph is at the top of its cycle,
so its instantaneous rate of change is zero right there.

</PredictReveal>

</EmbedCard>
