# Elementary Derivative Rules (Section 2.1)

Up to this point, a derivative has usually come from a graph, a table, or a
limit. Section 2.1 starts the algebra toolkit. The goal is not to memorize a
list in isolation. The goal is to look at a formula and know which parts you can
differentiate immediately.

<EmbedCard anchor="notation" title="Notation is part of the skill" tone="note">

The same derivative can be written several ways.

$$
f'(x)
$$

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)\right]
$$

Read the last one as an instruction: take the derivative, with respect to
$x$, of whatever is inside the brackets. That instruction notation is useful
because it keeps the rule visible while you work.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="first-rules" title="The first rules">

You should leave this section able to use these without starting from the limit
definition each time.

1. Constants have derivative zero.
2. Powers follow the power rule.
3. Constant multiples pass through the derivative.
4. Sums and differences differentiate term by term.
5. Exponential functions have their own rule.

In symbols:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[c\right] = 0
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^n\right] = n x^{n-1}
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[k f(x)\right] = k f'(x)
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x) + g(x)\right] = f'(x) + g'(x)
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[a^x\right] = a^x \ln(a)
$$

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard
  anchor="roots-and-reciprocals"
  title="Rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers"
  tone="quiet"
>

The power rule also handles negative and fractional exponents. Rewrite first,
then differentiate.

$$
\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{x}\right]
= \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}
= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{x}\right]
= -x^{-2}
= -\frac{1}{x^2}
$$

The variable's location decides the rule. $x^5$ is a power function, so the
power rule applies. $5^x$ is exponential, so its derivative is
$5^x\ln(5)$. Do not bring the exponent down when the variable is in the
exponent.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="worked-example" title="Worked example: one term at a time" tone="warn">

Suppose

$$
g(x) = 4x^7 - 3x^2 + 8
$$

Do not try to do the whole expression at once. Break it into terms.

**First term.** The derivative of $x^7$ is $7x^6$, and the constant multiple
$4$ stays out front, so $4x^7$ becomes $28x^6$.

**Second term.** The derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$, and the coefficient $-3$
stays out front, so $-3x^2$ becomes $-6x$.

**Third term.** The derivative of the constant $8$ is $0$.

So

$$
g'(x) = 28x^6 - 6x
$$

Notice what did not happen: the exponent did not stay the same, and the
constant term did not survive.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard
  anchor="tangent-line"
  title="Worked example: use the rules to build a tangent line"
  tone="plain"
>

Let

$$
p(x) = x^3 - 2x + 1
$$

and find the tangent line at $x = 2$.

**Find the point.**

$$
p(2) = 2^3 - 2(2) + 1 = 5
$$

**Find the slope.** Differentiate the formula, then evaluate the derivative.

$$
p'(x) = 3x^2 - 2
$$

$$
p'(2) = 3(2)^2 - 2 = 10
$$

**Build the line.** Use the point $(2,5)$ and slope $10$.

$$
y = 5 + 10(x - 2)
$$

Section 1.8 supplied the tangent-line formula. Section 2.1 now gives you a fast
way to calculate the slope that goes into it.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="common-trap" title="The constant term disappears" tone="quiet">

The derivative measures rate of change. A constant does not change, so its rate
of change is zero.

If $f(x) = x^4 + 12$, then

$$
f'(x) = 4x^3
$$

not $4x^3 + 12$. The $12$ affects the height of the graph, but not its slope.

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="predict" title="Predict, then check" tone="quiet">

Answer each one in your head first, then open it.

<PredictReveal question="What is the derivative of 9x^5?">

$45x^4$. The power rule changes $x^5$ to $5x^4$, and the coefficient $9$
stays out front: $9 \cdot 5x^4 = 45x^4$.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="What is the derivative of x^3 - 7x + 11?">

$3x^2 - 7$. The derivative of $x^3$ is $3x^2$, the derivative of $-7x$ is
$-7$, and the derivative of the constant $11$ is zero.

</PredictReveal>

<PredictReveal question="Which is a power function and which is exponential: x^5 or 5^x?">

$x^5$ is a power function because the variable is in the base. $5^x$ is
exponential because the variable is in the exponent. They do not use the same
derivative rule.

</PredictReveal>

</EmbedCard>

<EmbedCard anchor="what-to-do" title="What to be able to do">

Given a polynomial or a simple exponential expression, identify the rule that
applies to each term and compute the derivative formula. Be able to rewrite
roots and reciprocals as powers, explain why a constant term disappears,
distinguish $x^n$ from $a^x$, and use a derivative value to build a tangent
line.
